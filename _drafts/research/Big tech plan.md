This is a plan for landing a role at Google, Microsoft, Apple or Amazon from where you are on 13 October 2026: 14 years in quality engineering, a bank payments platform on your current résumé, strong AI tooling work, St. Thomas, Ontario, a full-time job that must not be put at risk, and a floor of CA$140K base (CA$160–170K to relocate to Toronto). It rests on three research tracks run today (live postings and interview loops at the five companies; what recruiters and hiring data actually say about candidates like you; SwiftGen recomputed from its own run log), plus my read of your LinkedIn, GitHub and portfolio site. The short version. The good news is that your profile is unusually close to two live postings, one of them remote, and that SwiftGen plus the eval harness give you something most candidates in that loop don't have: a measured, honestly reported AI system of your own. The plan is therefore: reposition as an SDET-flavoured software engineer, apply to the two close fits now with referrals, and spend the next twelve weeks on the coding bar so the loops you earn in January are winnable.

## What the loop tests, and what it doesn't

*Official.* Google: structured interviews against shared rubrics, a hiring assessment and coding exercise, one or two recruiter calls, optional project work, a panel, "roughly 6–8 weeks", AI tools banned in interviews, at most three applications per 30 days, and Canadian postings say in-person interviews are usual. Amazon SDE: a 90-minute online assessment (two coding problems, a 20-minute design section, work-style questions), then four 55-minute interviews, with a decision within five business days. Microsoft: "2–4 conversations"; candidate reports put it at four or five rounds of medium-difficulty coding with system design at SDE II and above, and offers about ten days after the loop (*Anecdote*).

*Data.* interviewing.io's study of about 10,000 interviews found 46% of people who received offers had no big-brand employer on their résumé; the non-brand signals that predicted success were quantified bullets, at most two typos, completed courses and teaching experience. GitHub activity was not one of them. The same source's October 2025 survey: no FAANG company has dropped algorithmic rounds, and 58% of interviewers changed their question style because of AI cheating. HackerRank 2025 (n = 13,732) and Karat 2025 (n = 400 hiring leaders) point the same way: live human interviews are tightening, not loosening.

*What this means.* SwiftGen, the eval harness and the bank tools are what get a recruiter or referrer to say yes to a screen. They do not get you through the loop. The loop is won by three months of deliberate coding practice in Java, and there is no sourced shortcut: the only published plan with numbers is the Tech Interview Handbook's roughly eleven hours a week for three months over the Grind 75 set (*Data*, one source). Starting now, that puts your first loops in January or February 2027 and offers, if they come, in the first half of 2027.

## SwiftGen, presented as a skill

*Data, recomputed today from the raw log of 499 runs.* Every number you've been using checks out: 220 of 333 generations succeeded end to end (66%), 71% over the last 100; first-pass success 43.5% overall and 53% over the last 100; 138 of 166 modifications succeeded (83%); the healer rescued 75 of 163 failed first builds (46%); median 141 seconds to a running app. Two things the recount added. First, a failure analysis nobody had done: of the 113 failed generations, 88 (78%) exhausted the three-attempt healer, and 25 (22%) never reached the compiler (nine unparseable outputs, fifteen schema failures on multi-screen apps). Second, a correction: the log records only Sonnet as the generating model, so "multi-model" must be framed as routing and fallback across Claude, GPT, Gemini and Grok, not as several models generating apps. Framing it the first way is what a careful interviewer would call out.

The skill is not "I built an app generator". The skill, in the words of the postings that want it, is building LLM workflows with verification gates and measuring them: a zero-LLM pre-validator with 193 patterns and a safety guard, a bounded repair loop that de-duplicates errors and escalates to a stronger model, a compiler and simulator used as the evaluator, an append-only run log, and a learning loop that promoted recurring repairs upstream and lifted first-pass success from 43.5% to 53%. Apple's SDET postings ask for exactly "applied LLMs to test generation, failure analysis, or coverage-gap identification"; Amazon's 2026 SDE postings ask for "agentic evaluation frameworks, monitoring, and feedback loops that ensure correctness"; Microsoft's remote role lists LLM evaluation as preferred. SwiftGen is the evidence for all three, as long as it is presented as measurement rather than product.

Ready to use, all numbers from the log, early-2026 models stated:

**Résumé, four bullets for an AI-heavy role.** SwiftGen AI, a multi-model LLM workflow with verification gates: idea to native SwiftUI, build, repair, launch in the iOS Simulator, edit by chat; solo, January to April 2026. Orchestration: intent and complexity classification routes each task across Claude Sonnet and Opus, GPT-4.1, Gemini 2.5 and Grok with per-task fallbacks, retries, truncation detection and timeouts; about 970 injected rules; schema-enforced screen-by-screen generation for apps with six or more screens. Verification of generated code: a zero-LLM pre-validator (193 patterns, about 15 cross-file fixers, a safety guard that rejects any fix that unbalances braces or deletes over half a file, explicit fixes for fabricated APIs), per-file typecheck, build, then launch, screenshot, process-alive and crash-log checks. Repair loop bounded at three attempts with de-duplicated errors and escalation to Opus; rescued 46% of failed first builds; every healer error logged (605, 431 fixed) and patterns seen five or more times at 80%+ success promoted upstream, lifting first-pass success from 43.5% to 53% on the last 100 runs. Measured from an append-only log of 499 runs: 66% end-to-end success (71% last 100), 83% modification success, median 2.4 minutes; 315 commits, about 70k lines of Python and 20k of TypeScript, about 130 tests.

**The 70-second spoken answer** ("tell me about a project you're proud of"). Situation: early this year I built SwiftGen alone; you describe an iOS app in a sentence and it generates native SwiftUI, compiles it, launches it in the simulator and lets you change it by chat. The hard part was not generation, it was stopping bad model output from reaching the user. Decisions, three: deterministic before paid, a regex pre-validator with 193 patterns that runs before any build and removes known failure classes including APIs the model invents; a bounded repair loop, at most three attempts, errors de-duplicated, the last attempt escalated to a stronger model; and an append-only log of every run so I could measure instead of guess. Result over 499 runs on early-2026 models: 66% end-to-end success, 71% over the last hundred, 83% on modifications; the repair loop rescued 46% of failed builds; and because recurring repairs were promoted into the validator, first-pass success rose from 43.5% to 53%. What I'd change: log which model did each repair, add a functional check beyond "it launched and didn't crash", and freeze a prompt set so re-runs on new models are comparable.

**The LinkedIn post, framed as a finding.** "An engineering finding from SwiftGen, my pipeline that turns a sentence into a built, running SwiftUI app. I logged every run: 499 of them, January to April 2026, early-2026 models. End-to-end success was 66%, 71% over the last 100. The number that taught me the most was the failure split. Of 113 failed generations, 88 reached the compiler and exhausted three model repair attempts. 25 never got there: the output could not be parsed into files, or a multi-screen app failed schema validation. Two different problems, two different fixes, and only the log told them apart. The repair loop rescued 46% of failed first builds. Useful, but every rescue is another model call. The cheaper lever was moving fixes upstream: errors reaching the healer were tracked (605 logged, 431 fixed) and recurring ones promoted into a zero-LLM pre-validator. First-pass success went from 43.5% overall to 53% on the last 100 runs. What I would not claim: that a passing build means a working app. The pipeline launches each app, screenshots it and scans crash logs, and that is still a weak check of behaviour. The harness is model-agnostic; these numbers are from that generation of models, not current ones."

**Evidence to publish.** Not the whole repository: it holds live secrets files, a server IP in the changelog, billing code, and documents whose claims ("Production Ready", "99.9% availability") the measured numbers contradict. Publish a scrubbed extract, "swiftgen-gates", with the six verification modules (pre-validator, healer, error tracker, runtime validator, crash monitor, logger), their tests, a README in the testgen-evals voice, and the run-log analysis as a Markdown report with the 60-line recompute script. Record one dated two-minute screen capture as a demonstration of the mechanism, not as evidence for the numbers. Re-run on current models only when you ask, and only after adding three log fields (healer model per attempt, complexity, failure stage) and freezing the prompt set.
